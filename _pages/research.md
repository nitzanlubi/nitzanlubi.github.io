To navigate complex environments, our brains must form lasting structured knowledge, while remaining plastic enough to update this knowledge as circumstances change. Disruptions to this balance can lead to cognitive rigidity – a difficulty to modify our structured knowledge and the internal models that rely on it. My research examines how the brain supports cognitive plasticity and how it can be enhanced: I use behavioral methods, time-resolved neuroimaging and decoding techniques to understand how the brain flexibly updates structured knowledge in naturalistic contexts. I also use closed-loop neurofeedback to modulate internal models, driving the brain toward more adaptive and healthy functioning.

## I. Why is information congruent with prior knowledge learned more easily?

Cognitive plasticity partly depends on our ability to integrate new information into our prior knowledge (schemas). Indeed, we never learn tabula rasa, yet how new learning is shaped by schemas remains unresolved. Schema-congruent associations are easier to learn, and fMRI has revealed where such learning occurs (hippocampal and cortical learning systems); but how the processing of congruent information differs, and why it benefits learning, remains open. I am testing a novel mechanistic theory, the representational coherence hypothesis, which proposes that schema congruency causes related stimuli to partially co-activate each other’s neural representations, yielding synchronized cortical dynamics during learning that support hippocampal encoding and improve memory recall. In this study, participants learn object–scene associations that are either congruent (e.g., a monkey in a jungle) or incongruent (e.g., a dolphin with a jungle) while undergoing either SQUID or next-generation OPM MEG scanning, with memory tested after a delay. Using temporal decoding and source localization, I compare the temporal alignment between object and scene representations during congruent vs incongruent learning and relate these dynamics to hippocampal encoding mechanisms.

## II. How does the brain control the immune system?

Brain and bodily functions are often treated separately, yet the placebo effect, where positive expectations may produce objective somatic changes, reveals how deeply they connect. In my doctoral research, I sought to uncover this causal link in humans by examining how the reward system primes the immune system for adversity (Lubianiker et al., 2026, *Nature Medicine*). Using an fMRI neurofeedback design consistent with my theoretical approach (Lubianiker et al., 2019, *Nature Human Behaviour*), I trained participants to upregulate the reward mesolimbic system, followed by administering Hepatitis B vaccine and immunological assessments. Testing this relationship in humans for the first time, I discovered that successful upregulation of the ventral tegmental area predicted a stronger immune response. Using a new quantitative tool I created to characterize the mental strategies underlying regulation, I found that participants achieved this by invoking positive expectations. This work reveals a potential neuropsychological mechanism for the placebo effect and establishes a brain-based approach for enhancing immunity.

## III. How can we learn to overcome negative attentional biases?

Individuals with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) show an inflexible attentional bias toward negative stimuli and thoughts. In my postdoctoral research, I examined whether the brain’s endogenous learning capacities can be harnessed to reduce such tendencies (Lubianiker et al., 2026, *medRxiv*). MDD patients viewed overlaid images of translucent negative faces and neutral scenes, and were instructed to attend to the scenes while ignoring the faces. Their attentional states were decoded in real time from fMRI and externalized through visual feedback, making the faces more visible when participants failed to ignore them. This increased awareness of the bias while reinforcing more balanced attention deployment. As hypothesized, training reduced the likelihood of focusing on faces compared to a sham control group. This work tests a neurocognitive mechanism underlying negative biases in depression, showing that it is possible to use real-time decoded neurofeedback to remediate an otherwise inflexible cognitive dysfunction.

## IV. Why are some people better at modulating their brain activity?

Neurofeedback, in my view, holds a vast potential as a mechanistic framework for cognitive neuroscientific research. To help establish it as such, I provided this method with firmer theoretical footing and greater experimental breadth. For example, I devised a computational framework for neurofeedback training (Lubianiker et al., 2022, *Trends in Neurosciences*), formalizing it as a closed-loop internal reinforcement learning problem, with testable algorithmic descriptions for identifying optimal training regimes. In addition, I laid out a process-based research scheme which (1) guides the translation of neuropsychological hypotheses into self-neuromodulation clinical interventions, (2) introduces a novel control condition separating genuine neuromodulation from non-specific placebo effects, and (3) offers new experimental designs (Lubianiker et al., 2019, *Nature Human Behaviour*). Finally, participants regulate brain activity by applying mental strategies, an overlooked aspect in current methodology. I developed the Mental Strategies Questionnaire (MSQ) and a complementary quantitative assessment pipeline, unlocking the possibility to link neural activity patterns to 46 mental features. Applying it to ~8,000 trials from seven pooled EEG and fMRI neurofeedback experiments, I uncovered new mental correlates of region-specific control: positive affect facilitated nucleus accumbens up-regulation, whereas low arousal, calmness and tactile imagery supported amygdala down-regulation (Lubianiker et al., 2026, in prep).
